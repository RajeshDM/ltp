\begin{abstract}

Learned policies for classical planning act without search, but each policy is tied to the domain it was trained on: network modules per action schema, parameters per predicate, or vocabulary-sized encodings mean a trained policy cannot even be executed on new predicates and action schemas. We present \gadar{} (Generalizing Across Domains via Action Ranking), a policy for classical planning that takes the domain description as input --- $\pi(s, G, D)$ --- and constructs grounded actions in domains never seen in training, zero-shot and without search. The capability is built in four cumulative steps, each a contribution and each the ablation of the next: (i) \emph{symbol-free structural features} replace symbol identity with structural signatures, making a single action-ranking policy executable in any domain; (ii) a \emph{lifted domain layer} embeds $D$ itself into every state graph, identifying predicates and action schemas by their declared roles --- which schema uses them, as precondition or effect, at which argument slots --- rather than by name; (iii) a \emph{binding layer} ties each ground action's applicability to the lifted precondition occurrences it instantiates, expressing search-free action construction in the vocabulary of the domain description; and (iv) a \emph{chain layer} makes the domain's own action structure explicit --- which schema produces what another schema needs, which schema destroys it, how far each schema stands from achieving the goal, and which applicable grounding would achieve an open goal atom or delete a true atom. The last step is what turns executability into transfer: on a domain held out of training entirely, \gadar{} lifts zero-shot coverage from 16\% to 100\% on training-scale instances, at a plan quality of 0.97 relative to a satisficing planner and with 99.2\% of its top-ranked action proposals valid, and it exceeds the ceiling of a hand-coded goal-achievement oracle, so what transfers is learned lifted structure rather than a terminal trigger. \gadar{} is invariant to symbol renaming by construction, and we show the binding layer strictly increases expressiveness over the lifted graphs used for cross-domain heuristic learning.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Classical planning tackles the problem of finding action sequences to achieve
goals in deterministic environments. While traditional planners can solve
small problems optimally using search and heuristics, they often struggle
with scalability on complex problems \citep{complexity-planning,
fast_downward}. This has motivated research into learning-based planning: a
model trained on solved problems captures regularities that carry over to new
ones. Two families of learned guidance have developed largely in parallel.
Learned \emph{heuristics} estimate cost-to-go and guide a search algorithm
\citep{strips_hgn, karia2021learning, goose_v2}; learned \emph{policies}
select actions directly, executing plans greedily without search
\citep{toyer2020asnets, expressive_v1, gabar}.

Learned policies have proven effective at generalizing \emph{within} a
domain. ASNets learn per-schema network modules that transfer across problem
sizes \citep{toyer2020asnets}; relational GNN policies learn value functions
whose greedy execution solves instances far larger than those seen in
training \citep{expressive_v1, policy_beyond_c2}; GABAR encodes states as
action-centric graphs and constructs grounded actions by ranking ---
selecting an action schema by scoring schema-node embeddings, then each
argument by scoring object-node embeddings --- generalizing to instances
significantly larger than its training problems \citep{gabar}. These policies
share a design choice: the domain's schemas and predicates are reflected in
the network's structure, whether as modules, parameters, or feature
dimensions. This is a strength for within-domain generalization --- the
network is shaped to its domain --- but it ties each trained policy to the
domain it was trained on: the policy cannot be executed on a domain whose
schemas and predicates have no slots in the network.

For learned heuristics, a different treatment of the domain has emerged.
STRIPS-HGN computes structural features that make no reference to symbol
identity \citep{strips_hgn}, and GOOSE's lifted learning graph (LLG) goes
further, encoding the full domain description --- schemas, preconditions,
effects, argument bindings --- as part of the heuristic's input
\citep{goose_v2}. In GOOSE's domain-independent setting, a single heuristic
is trained on one collection of domains and evaluated on held-out ones. This
line of work establishes that the \emph{input} side of learning for planning
can be made domain-agnostic. Heuristics, however, delegate action
construction to search, and the corresponding step for policies --- which
must construct grounded actions themselves --- remains open; \citet{goose_v2}
identify lifted policy learning as future work. It is a natural next step,
and a nontrivial one: the same authors prove that message-passing networks
over the lifted graph cannot compute even basic heuristics such as
$h^{\mathrm{add}}$, and their domain-independent configuration is empirically
their most difficult setting.

In this work we take that step. We learn a policy $\pi(s, G, D)$ that
receives the domain description $D$ as input alongside the state $s$ and goal
$G$, and constructs grounded actions in domains never seen in training ---
zero-shot, without search. Two established routes frame the contribution:
classical planners are domain-general but buy that generality with search at
plan time, while learned policies execute reactively but specialize to one
domain at training time. $\pi(s, G, D)$ occupies the remaining corner --- a
single learned artifact that is both domain-general and search-free --- and
the question we study is what such a policy can transfer, not whether it
outraces search. Our key idea is to change what the network is told
about symbols: not \emph{which symbol} something is, but \emph{what role the
symbol plays} in the lifted domain --- to bind every ground action's
applicability to that lifted structure, and to make the domain's own action
structure --- which schema produces what another schema needs, and how far
each schema stands from the goal --- an explicit part of the input, so that
the ranking decoder can score actions over symbols it has never seen.

We build this capability as a \emph{ladder} of four systems, each adding
exactly one representational commitment to the previous one, so that every
adjacent comparison isolates one component (the suffix names the last
component a system contains; full \gadar{} is the top rung). \union{} is the
control: GABAR's symbol-identity features widened to the union of the
training domains' vocabularies --- multi-task learning with the domain in the
weights. \gadarstruct{} replaces symbol identity with structural features
computable in any domain, making the policy \emph{executable} on unseen
domains. \gadarbind{} adds the domain description to every state graph --- a
lifted domain layer of predicate-symbol and occurrence nodes, plus the
\emph{grounded--lifted binding layer} that annotates each applicable ground
action with links to the lifted precondition occurrences it instantiates. The
binding layer connects what the two prior lines each capture only partially
--- lifted domain structure on one side, ground applicability on the other
--- and we prove the combination is strictly more expressive than the lifted
graph alone: message-passing networks on the binding-layer graph distinguish
planning tasks whose optimal actions differ but that are indistinguishable on
LLG (Proposition~1). Expressiveness, however, is not yet transfer: a network
can only exploit domain structure that message passing actually reaches, and
the relation ``schema $A$ produces what schema $B$ consumes'' is, in the
binding-layer graph, two hops through a shared predicate hub that loses the
pairing. Full \gadar{} therefore adds the \emph{chain layer}: edges between
the occurrence nodes themselves (\emph{enables}, \emph{threatens}), a
per-schema goal distance computed by backward search over those edges,
goal-relevance marks on objects, and grounded effect edges from an applicable
grounding to the goal atoms it would achieve and the true atoms it would
delete. Every one of these is computed from $D$ and the goal alone, is binary
or one-hot, and refers to no symbol name --- gripper's pick--drop and
miconic's board--depart are, to this layer, the same object. Empirically this
last rung is what converts executability into transfer. Downstream of the
representation, all four systems share GABAR's encoder, ranking decoder, and
search-free execution unchanged, so every comparison varies the
representation rather than the architecture. The ranking objective, one
design choice among several in the single-domain setting, does additional
work here: rankings are scale-free across domains, whereas value functions
presuppose a scale shared between domains --- a point independently
corroborated for learned heuristics and Q-value policies
\citep{chrestien_optimize, mueller_qvalues}.

Our experiments follow a leave-one-domain-out protocol over the eight GABAR
benchmark domains, with four representative domains --- Blocks, Gripper,
Miconic, and Visitall --- evaluated as held-out targets. On Visitall, the
first target for which the top two rungs have been measured, a policy trained on
the other seven domains and never exposed to Visitall's predicates, schemas,
or objects solves 100\% of training-scale instances zero-shot, against 16\%
for the same system without the chain layer (\gadarbind{}) and
\todonum{7/7 solved so far} against a 6\% random floor on the large test
instances. It does so at plan quality 0.97 relative to a satisficing planner
--- near-planner-length plans, not lucky wandering --- with 99.2\% of its
top-ranked proposals valid, and above the 50\% ceiling of a hand-coded oracle
that always prefers an applicable action directly satisfying an open goal
atom, which rules out the reading that the transfer is a terminal trigger in
disguise. The remaining held-out targets, the union-vocabulary control, and
the intermediate rungs on those targets are in flight and reported as
placeholders; we state plainly that the capability claim currently rests on
one fully measured held-out domain. By construction, \gadar{} is invariant to renaming of
predicates, schemas, and objects --- a robustness that symbol-based
approaches, including current large reasoning models, do not share
\citep{llm_baseline}

--- and the same invariance yields a precise transfer ceiling: structurally
isomorphic domains with different semantics necessarily receive identical

\textbf{Contribution.} We present the first domain-agnostic policy for
classical planning: zero-shot grounded-action construction in unseen
domains, without search. The capability is delivered as a ladder of systems
in which each addition --- structural features, the lifted domain layer with
its grounded--lifted binding layer, and the chain layer --- is certified by
the comparison to its predecessor, so the method and its ablation study are
the same object. The binding layer is what makes ground applicability
expressible in the domain's own vocabulary, and is justified theoretically by
a strict expressiveness separation from the lifted graphs of prior work; the
chain layer, built on top of it, is what makes that structure usable by
message passing and is the component that empirically delivers transfer.

\textbf{Learning heuristics across domains.} Learning domain-independent
heuristics has a long history; we focus on the neural line. STRIPS-HGN trains
hypergraph networks on structural features of the delete relaxation and
evaluates on domains unseen in training \citep{strips_hgn}. GOOSE introduces
graph representations of planning tasks for both grounded and lifted inputs;
its lifted learning graph encodes the domain description itself --- schemas,
preconditions, effects, and argument bindings via index features --- and its
domain-independent setting trains a single heuristic across many domains and
evaluates on held-out ones \citep{goose_v1, goose_v2}. WL-GOOSE obtains
stronger per-domain heuristics with classical machine learning over
Weisfeiler--Lehman features, at the cost of per-domain feature dictionaries
\citep{wl_goose}. \citet{karia2021learning} learn generalized heuristics over
canonical abstractions without requiring the model at evaluation time. These
works establish domain-agnostic \emph{inputs}, and we adopt their lesson ---
structural features and lifted domain graphs. They differ from our setting in
their output: a heuristic delegates action construction to search, which
remains in the execution loop. \citet{goose_v2} additionally prove an
expressiveness limitation of message passing on their lifted graph and name
lifted policy learning as an open problem; our binding layer is designed
precisely to add the information that a policy needs and their graph
provably lacks (Proposition~1).

\textbf{Learned policies for planning.} Generalized policy learning aims at
policies that transfer across instances of a domain
\citep{approx_Policy_iter, relational_rl}. ASNets construct a network from
the domain's action schemas and ground atoms, with weight sharing across
instantiations of the same schema \citep{toyer2020asnets}. St{\aa}hlberg,
Bonet, and Geffner learn value functions with GNNs whose greedy policies
generalize across instance sizes, characterize their expressive power in
terms of two-variable logics, and extend the approach with policy gradients
and unsupervised objectives \citep{expressive_v1, unsupervised_gnn,
policy_gradients, policy_beyond_c2}. Relational deep RL approaches
similarly learn policies over relational state representations
\citep{relational_drl, sr_drl, relational_mdp_learning, rddl_gnn,
generalized_planning_deep_rl}. GABAR, which this work extends, represents
ground actions explicitly in the state graph and constructs actions
autoregressively by ranking \citep{gabar}. In all of these, the domain's
vocabulary is reflected in the network --- as per-schema modules,
per-predicate parameters, or vocabulary-sized encodings --- so the trained
policy is bound to its training domain. \gadar{} removes this binding by
moving the domain description from the parameters of the network into its
input.

\textbf{Ranking in planning.} Learning to rank has been used to order
states and heuristic values for search: linear ranking functions for beam
search \citep{ranking_old_2009}, RankSVM-based heuristic synthesis
\citep{ranking_og}, ranking-optimized heuristics \citep{chrestien_optimize},
and learned pairwise rankings for greedy best-first search \citep{gbfs_v2}.
GABAR instead ranks \emph{actions} locally within a state, avoiding the need
for a globally consistent function \citep{gabar}. In the cross-domain
setting this choice becomes load-bearing rather than convenient: a value
function shared across domains presupposes a common scale of goal distances
that does not exist, whereas local action rankings are scale-free.
\citet{mueller_qvalues} provide independent convergent evidence from the
single-domain setting: vanilla Q-value regression fails precisely because it
does not discriminate between actions, and is repaired by a ranking-style
margin objective.

\textbf{Large language models and planning.} LLM-based planners have been
evaluated extensively; systematic studies find that performance of large
reasoning models degrades sharply when symbols are renamed
\citep{llm_baseline}, indicating reliance on symbol identity. \gadar{} is
invariant to symbol renaming by construction. LLM code-synthesis approaches
that write per-domain programs require solved instances and validator loops
at test time, addressing a different question from zero-shot execution.

\section{Background and Problem Setup}

A classical planning problem is a pair $P = \langle D, I \rangle$, where $D$
is a first-order domain and $I$ contains instance-specific information. The
domain $D$ consists of a set of predicate symbols $\mathcal{P}$ with
associated arities and a set of action schemas $\mathcal{A}$. Each schema $a
\in \mathcal{A}$ is defined by a tuple of parameters $\Delta(a)$ representing
variables that can be instantiated, preconditions $pre(a)$, add effects
$add(a)$, and delete effects $del(a)$. The instance information $I$ is a
tuple $\langle O, s_0, G \rangle$, where $O$ is a finite set of objects,
$s_0$ is the initial state represented as a set of ground atoms, and $G$ is
the goal condition, also a set of ground atoms. A ground action $a(o_1,
\dots, o_k)$ substitutes objects for a schema's parameters; it is applicable
in state $s$ if $pre(a) \subseteq s$ under the substitution, and yields
successor $s' = (s \setminus del(a)) \cup add(a)$. A plan is a sequence of
applicable ground actions transforming $s_0$ into a state satisfying $G$.

GABAR addresses the following problem: given a domain $D$ and solved training
instances of different sizes, learn a relational policy $\pi_D(s, G)$ that
solves larger test instances of the \emph{same} domain \citep{gabar}. This
paper addresses the cross-domain version. Given a set of training domains
$D_1, \dots, D_n$, each with solved instances of small problems, learn a
\emph{single} policy $\pi(s, G, D)$ --- taking the domain description itself
as input --- that solves instances of a test domain $D_{\mathit{test}}
\notin \{D_1, \dots, D_n\}$, zero-shot. Execution is search-free and follows
GABAR's protocol: the highest-ranked applicable action is executed, a
monitor optionally avoids revisiting states, and execution ends at the goal,
at a dead end, or at a length bound.

\section{GADAR}

Given a planning instance in PDDL format together with its domain
description $D$, \gadar{} converts the pair into a graph, processes the
graph with a GNN encoder, and constructs a grounded action with a GRU-based
decoder that first selects an action schema and then each of its arguments
(Figure~\ref{fig:system}). The encoder and decoder are those of GABAR,
unchanged; what changes is the graph they operate on. We present the
representation as it is evaluated: a ladder of systems, each adding one
commitment --- structural features in place of symbol identity, then the
lifted domain layer with its binding layer, then the chain layer --- so that
each addition is certified by comparison with its predecessor.

\subsection{A Domain-Agnostic Graph Representation}

\textbf{Ground instance layer.} The base of the representation is GABAR's
action-centric graph \citep{gabar}: nodes for objects, for ground literals
of the current state and the goal (goal literals marked by a flag), and for
action schemas, together with a global node; edges connect literals to their
argument objects and schemas to the objects of their applicable groundings,
with parameter positions on the edges (Figure~\ref{fig:representation},
bottom). This graph is what makes search-free action construction possible:
the decoder selects among schema nodes and object nodes that are present in
the input.

\textbf{Structural features (\gadarstruct{}).} GABAR's node features identify
symbols: one-hot encodings over the domain's schemas, predicates, and types,
so feature dimensions --- and therefore network weights --- are sized by the
domain vocabulary. \gadar{} replaces every symbol-identity feature with
structural ones computable in any domain: the node's kind (object, literal,
schema, symbol, global), the arity of its predicate or schema, whether the
predicate is static, whether a literal belongs to the goal, and argument
positions encoded as edge features rather than per-domain position
vocabularies. Feature widths are now constants independent of the domain, so
a single network serves every domain, and permuting symbol names leaves the
input tensors unchanged --- renaming invariance holds by construction
(Proposition~2). The system that stops here is \gadarstruct{}: executable in
any domain, but informed about $D$ only through these local signatures.

\textbf{Lifted domain layer.} Structural features alone
discard information that distinguishes symbols playing different roles.
\gadar{} restores that information by embedding the domain description
itself in every state graph (Figure~\ref{fig:representation}, top): one
\emph{symbol node} per predicate, connected to the ground layer by
\emph{instantiation edges} from each ground literal to its predicate's
symbol node, and to each schema node by \emph{role edges} recording how the
predicate appears in that schema --- as precondition, add, or delete effect
--- and at which positions, with the correspondence between predicate
arguments and schema parameter slots carried as edge features. A predicate
is then identified not by name but by its signature in $D$: which schemas
consume it, in which roles, at which slots. This layer is LLG-adjacent, and
we credit its lineage: GOOSE first demonstrated that the full lifted domain
can be an input for learning \citep{goose_v2}. Our construction differs in
engineering --- argument positions as edge features rather than chains of
index nodes, undirected message flow, and a retained global node --- but its
role differs more fundamentally, as we describe next.

\textbf{The grounded--lifted binding layer (\gadarbind{}).} The lifted layer
describes what the domain's actions \emph{could} do; the ground layer
describes what \emph{can be done now}. Neither prior representation connects
the two: LLG contains lifted preconditions but no ground actions, while
GABAR annotates its schema--object edges with precondition-satisfaction
flags indexed by per-domain strings that do not transfer. The binding layer
makes the connection explicit, and in a vocabulary that exists in every
domain --- the domain description's own. Full \gadar{} first refines the
lifted layer with \emph{occurrence nodes}: one node per occurrence of a
predicate in a schema's preconditions or effects, so that two occurrences of
the same predicate in one schema remain distinct, each carrying its role and
its exact argument-to-slot correspondence. Then, for each applicable ground
action, its schema--object edges are annotated with \emph{binding edges} to
the lifted precondition-occurrence nodes they instantiate: ``object $o$ at
parameter slot $j$ satisfies precondition occurrence $p$ of schema $a$''
(Figure~\ref{fig:representation}, green). Together with the instantiation
edges, these let the network reason about why an action is applicable and
what it will change, using only nodes that exist for any domain. In the
domain of Figure~\ref{fig:representation}, the ground edge placing O3 in
Unstack's first slot binds to the occurrences \emph{on(x,y)} and
\emph{clear(x)} --- information that survives unchanged if every symbol in
the domain is renamed. We show in Proposition~1 that this addition is not
merely convenient: message-passing networks on the binding-layer graph
distinguish tasks that are provably indistinguishable on the lifted graph
alone. The system that stops here --- lifted layer plus binding layer --- is
\gadarbind{}.

\textbf{The chain layer (\gadar{}).} \gadarbind{} contains the domain's
structure but leaves the network to rediscover, in every state and for every
domain, the single relation that planning turns on: that one schema produces
what another schema needs. In \gadarbind{} that relation exists only as a
two-hop path through the shared predicate-symbol node, and the intermediate
hub aggregates over all its occurrences, so the pairing --- \emph{which}
producer feeds \emph{which} consumer --- is exactly what the aggregation
destroys. The chain layer states the relation directly. It has four
ingredients, all computed from the domain description and the goal condition
alone, all binary or one-hot, and none referring to a symbol's name.

\emph{(i) Chaining edges between occurrence nodes.} For every predicate $p$,
we add an \emph{enables} edge from each add-occurrence of $p$ in a schema $A$
to each precondition-occurrence of $p$ in a schema $B$ (``$A$ produces what
$B$ needs''), and a \emph{threatens} edge from each delete-occurrence of $p$
in $A$ to each precondition-occurrence of $p$ in $B$ (``$A$ destroys what $B$
needs''). Because the endpoints are occurrences rather than predicates, the
producer--consumer pairing survives message passing intact, and delete
effects --- otherwise inert in the representation --- acquire a consequence.
This is where the cross-domain invariant lives: gripper's
$\textsf{pick}\!\rightarrow\!\textsf{drop}$ and miconic's
$\textsf{board}\!\rightarrow\!\textsf{depart}$ are the \emph{same} lifted
structure, an enables edge between an add-occurrence and a
precondition-occurrence of a shared predicate, and a network that has learned
the pattern in one domain recognizes it in the other. The previous graph
could not see it as one object at all.

\emph{(ii) Per-schema goal distance.} Over the enables relation we run a
backward breadth-first search from the schemas that add a goal predicate:
distance $0$ for those, distance $d{+}1$ for a schema that enables a schema
at distance $d$, clamped at $3{+}$, which is also the bucket for schemas that
never reach the goal. The result is attached to each schema node as a
four-way one-hot ($0/1/2/3{+}$) together with an \emph{adds-goal-predicate}
flag at distance $0$. It depends only on \emph{which} predicates occur in the
goal, not on the current state, so it is computed once per (domain, goal
signature) pair and reused for every state of every instance sharing that
signature: the per-state cost is zero.

\emph{(iii) Goal-relevant object marks.} Objects that occur in a goal atom
not yet satisfied in the current state receive a binary mark, computed by a
single pass over the open goal atoms. This and the grounded effect edges
below are the layer's only state-dependent ingredients.

\emph{(iv) Grounded effect edges.} The three ingredients above are lifted;
the last one grounds them. For each applicable grounding we substitute its
parameter bindings into its schema's effects and check the result against the
instance: an add effect equal to an \emph{unsatisfied} goal atom yields
\emph{achieves-goal} edges from the grounding's objects to that goal-literal
node, and a delete effect equal to a \emph{true} state atom yields
\emph{deletes-true} edges to that literal node. The decoder scores object
nodes, so these edges put ``choosing this object here closes an open goal''
and ``choosing it undoes something currently true'' one hop from the quantity
being ranked.

Every value the chain layer adds is a flag or a one-hot; nothing is a
magnitude. The goal distance in particular is bucketed rather than emitted as
a number, deliberately: a numeric distance would have to be read on a scale,
and a scale shared across domains is precisely what the cross-domain setting
does not provide --- the same argument that makes local action ranking, and
not value regression, the right objective here.

\textbf{Relation to GOOSE's LLG.} Since our contribution is a graph, we
state precisely how it differs from the closest prior graph
(Table~\ref{tab:llg}). LLG and our lifted layer agree on the essential
idea --- the domain description as input --- and differ in encoding
(argument positions as edge features rather than chains of index nodes;
occurrence nodes carrying exact argument-to-slot pairs). The fundamental
difference is what the graph is \emph{for}. A heuristic graph must support
one number, read from a global embedding; LLG therefore contains no ground
actions and no applicability information, and \citet{goose_v2} prove that
message passing on it cannot even compute $h^{\mathrm{add}}$. A policy
graph must contain the decoder's targets --- schema nodes and object nodes
to score --- and the evidence for scoring them: which groundings are
applicable and why. GABAR showed the first half (ground actions in the
graph) with per-domain annotations that do not transfer; the binding layer
supplies the second half in $D$'s own vocabulary, and Proposition~1 shows
the addition strictly increases what message passing can distinguish.

\subsection{A Ladder of Domain Conditioning}

The four systems we evaluate stand in a strict subset relation, each the
previous plus exactly one representational commitment:

\begin{itemize}
\item \union{} (control): GABAR's symbol-identity one-hots, widened to the
  union of the training domains' vocabularies. The domain stays in the
  weights; this is multi-task learning, not domain conditioning.
\item \gadarstruct{}: symbol-free structural features replace the one-hots.
  The policy becomes executable in any domain.
\item \gadarbind{}: plus the lifted domain layer --- predicate-symbol nodes,
  occurrence nodes, instantiation and role edges --- and the binding edges
  that tie each applicable grounding's slots to the precondition occurrences
  it satisfies. Ground applicability is expressed in the vocabulary of $D$.
\item \gadar{}: plus the chain layer --- enables and threatens edges between
  occurrence nodes, per-schema goal distance, goal-relevant object marks, and
  grounded achieves-goal / deletes-true edges. The domain's own action
  structure, and its relation to the goal, become explicit.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Because adjacent systems differ in exactly one component, the
experimental comparison and the ablation study are the same object: the
\union{}-to-\gadarstruct{} step measures what symbol identity was worth and
what executability costs, the \gadarstruct{}-to-\gadarbind{} step measures
the value of expressing ground applicability in the domain's own vocabulary
--- the component Proposition~1 argues is necessary --- and the
\gadarbind{}-to-\gadar{} step isolates the chain layer, which makes that
structure reachable by message passing. All four share one encoder input
convention: feature widths are fixed constants across domains (capped
maximum predicate and schema arities), so instances from different domains
mix freely within a training batch. The lifted layer adds a per-state cost
of domain-sized constants --- one symbol node per predicate, occurrence
nodes per schema literal --- plus one instantiation edge per ground
literal. The chain layer adds no node and no per-state computation beyond
the goal-relevance marks and the effect edges of applicable groundings: its
chaining edges and goal distances are functions of $(D, \text{goal
signature})$ and are computed once and cached. We report measured per-state
inference times alongside coverage.

\subsection{Action Construction and Training}

The decoder is GABAR's and we change it only where per-domain constants
would otherwise appear (Figure~\ref{fig:system}d). From the encoded graph,
a GRU selects an action schema by scoring the schema-node embeddings against
its hidden state, then selects each argument in turn by scoring object-node
embeddings, feeding each selection back as the next input. The argument loop
runs to the \emph{selected schema's} arity, read from the domain
description, under a global maximum-arity cap --- not a per-domain constant.
No component of the decoder is sized by the number of schemas, objects, or
predicates; selection is always a dot product between the decoder state and
the embedding of a node present in the input graph. This output-lifted
decoder is what allows the same network to construct actions in a domain
whose schemas it has never seen.

Training is supervised, as in GABAR: expert plans are collected with a
classical planner on small instances of each training domain, and the policy
is trained with cross-entropy to reproduce the expert's schema choice and
each argument choice at every state along the plans. Because feature widths
are domain-independent, instances from different domains mix freely within a
batch. The objective ranks actions \emph{locally within a state}, which is
what permits a shared network across domains: local rankings are scale-free,
while a value target would presuppose a scale of goal distances shared
between domains \citep{chrestien_optimize, mueller_qvalues}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis}

Two properties frame what the representation can and cannot do. The first
separates the binding-layer graph from the lifted graph alone; the second
states the invariance that enables transfer and, in the same breath, its
ceiling.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Proposition 1} (expressiveness separation).
\emph{There exist pairs of planning tasks $(s, G, D)$ and $(s', G', D')$
whose sets of optimal actions differ, such that every message-passing
network produces identical outputs on their lifted graphs
$\mathrm{LLG}(s,G,D)$ and $\mathrm{LLG}(s',G',D')$, while message-passing
networks on the corresponding binding-layer graphs distinguish them.}
\end{quote}

\noindent The construction adapts the counterexample family used by
\citet{goose_v2} to show that MPNNs on LLG cannot compute $h^{\mathrm{add}}$
or $h^{\mathrm{max}}$: their task pairs are symmetric to message passing on
the lifted graph while differing in heuristic value. We adapt the pairs so
that they differ in which ground actions are applicable and optimal, and
show that the binding annotations --- which tie each applicable ground
action's slots to the precondition occurrences they satisfy --- break the
symmetry that message passing cannot otherwise resolve.
% TODO (Phase 5): full construction and proof in the appendix; verify the
% adapted pairs against the exact statement of GOOSE Thm 4.3.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Proposition 2} (renaming invariance and its ceiling).
\emph{$\pi(s, G, D)$ is invariant under isomorphisms of the domain
description: for any bijective renaming $\sigma$ of predicates, schemas,
types, and objects, $\pi(\sigma(s), \sigma(G), \sigma(D)) =
\sigma(\pi(s, G, D))$. Consequently, if two domains are structurally
isomorphic but semantically different, the policy necessarily behaves
identically on them.}
\end{quote}

\noindent Invariance follows by construction: no feature in the
representation encodes symbol identity, so renamed inputs produce identical
tensors up to node permutation, and the encoder and decoder are permutation
equivariant. The consequence is a precise transfer ceiling rather than an
empirical failure mode: zero-shot behavior on a new domain is determined by
structure alone, so domains whose structures coincide receive the same
policy regardless of what their symbols mean. Distinguishing them requires
data from the new domain; charting that few-shot territory is future work.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Domains.} We evaluate on the eight benchmark domains used by GABAR
--- Blocks, Miconic, Spanner, Gripper, Visitall, Grid, Logistics, and
Rovers \citep{gabar, pddl-generators}. Training data consists of plans
computed by a classical planner \citep{fast_downward} on the small instances
of each training domain (between \todonum{125} and \todonum{312} instances
per domain); states along each plan are converted to graphs and the policy
is trained to reproduce the expert action as described above. As held-out
targets we report four representative domains --- Blocks, Gripper, Miconic,
and Visitall --- chosen because each has a distinct documented failure mode
for a reactive policy and a separately measured random floor, so that the
zero-shot numbers of every system are matched on the same four splits. Our
Blocks instances come from the many-blocks generator family
\citep{pddl-generators}.

\textbf{Protocol.} Three experiment families, all run for every rung of the
ladder. (i)~\emph{Leave-one-domain-out} (the headline): for each target
domain, a single policy is trained on the remaining seven and evaluated on
the held-out domain zero-shot, having never seen its predicates, schemas, or
objects. (ii)~\emph{In-domain}: one policy trained on all eight domains and
evaluated on each, measuring what a shared domain-conditioned policy costs
relative to per-domain training (the price of generality).
(iii)~\emph{Training-set diversity}: the full system additionally trained
on four and on six of the eight domains and evaluated zero-shot on domains
held out at every size, tracing how transfer scales with the diversity of
the training collection. Leave-one-domain-out is stricter than the single
fixed train/held-out split used in prior domain-independent evaluation
\citep{goose_v2}. Held-out domains are
evaluated on two splits: the \emph{hard} split is the domain's test set,
with instances up to an order of magnitude larger than training-scale
problems; the \emph{easy} split consists of the held-out domain's own
training-scale instances --- unseen by the policy, since the domain itself
was held out --- separating ``cannot handle the unseen domain'' from
``cannot scale within it.'' Training domains are evaluated on the hard
split only.

\textbf{Baselines and references.} Our primary comparison is the \union{}
control described above: identical data, architecture, training procedure,
and budget --- only the representation differs. Any zero-shot capability
\gadar{} has beyond this control is attributable to moving the domain into
the input. Two references bracket the zero-shot numbers. The \emph{floor} is
a random policy, and we hold it to strict protocol parity: it runs the same
executor, the same revisit monitor, and the same step bound as every learned
system, and the only difference is that the ranking over the applicable
ground actions is replaced by a uniform random sample. It therefore measures
exactly what the harness achieves when the learned component contributes
nothing. This matters because the floor is not uniformly low: on the easy
split of Visitall it is 99.73\%, since a monitored random walk on a
connected grid approximates optimal exploration, and it is 88.30\% on easy
Miconic. Visitall must accordingly be read on the hard split (floor 6.00\%)
and on plan quality, where a random walk is not competitive; the easy-split
Visitall number certifies executability, not competence. The \emph{ceiling} is published
per-domain GABAR \citep{gabar}, trained directly on each held-out domain; it
sees the test domain in training and by construction cannot be executed
zero-shot, so it calibrates --- rather than competes with --- transfer, and
we read zero-shot coverage as the fraction of this ceiling that transfer
recovers. Finally, the satisficing planner that generates our training data
\citep{fast_downward} solves \todonum{$\approx$100}\% of the test instances
--- by search. As in all prior work on generalized policies, the planner is
the plan-quality reference, not a competitor: Table~\ref{tab:related}
separates the two classes, and the object of study is what a reactive,
search-free policy can transfer.

\textbf{Ablations.} The ladder is the ablation study
(Figure~\ref{fig:system}b): \gadarbind{} is exactly \gadar{} with the chain
layer removed, and \gadarstruct{} is exactly \gadarbind{} with the lifted
domain layer and its binding edges removed. No separate ablation systems are
needed; every component's contribution is read off adjacent columns of
Table~\ref{table:main_table}, and all systems are evaluated on the same four
held-out splits. We additionally report one diagnostic that is not a rung of
the ladder: an \emph{achievement oracle} that, at every state, prefers any
applicable ground action whose add effects directly satisfy an unsatisfied
goal atom, and otherwise defers to the policy. It upper-bounds what a
hand-coded terminal trigger can buy on each domain and thereby separates
``the network learned to fire when the goal is one step away'' from ``the
network learned to navigate toward the goal.''

\textbf{Metrics and implementation.} Following GABAR, we report coverage
(C), the percentage of test problems solved within the execution bound, and
plan quality (P), the ratio of the reference planner's plan length to the
policy's on jointly solved problems, so higher is better and values above 1
indicate plans shorter than the reference. Because P conditions on the
problems a system solves, systems with higher coverage are scored on harder
instances; coverage is therefore the primary metric and P is read within,
not across, columns. For the zero-shot analysis we add a third,
construction-level metric: \emph{top-1 proposal validity} (V1), the fraction
of decoding steps at which the policy's highest-ranked constructed action is
applicable in the current state. V1 separates the two ways a transferred
policy can fail --- building a syntactically well-formed but inapplicable
action, versus building applicable actions that go nowhere --- and it is
defined even on problems the policy does not solve. Encoder and decoder
hyperparameters are carried over from GABAR unchanged ($L{=}9$
message-passing rounds, 64-dimensional representations, GRU decoder); all
systems train for 500 epochs with batch size 64 and learning rate
$5{\times}10^{-4}$. Checkpoints are selected by combined training and
validation loss, and every saved checkpoint of every run is evaluated; the
test set plays no role in model selection. The reason for the second half of
that protocol is empirical and we report it in the results: zero-shot
transfer is not monotone in training progress.

\textbf{Zero-shot transfer to an unseen domain.} The headline result is the
Visitall row of Table~\ref{table:main_table}. A single policy trained on the
other seven benchmark domains --- never exposed to Visitall's predicates,
schemas, or objects --- solves 100.0\% of the 125 training-scale instances of
the held-out domain, against 16.0\% for \gadarbind{}, the same system with
the chain layer removed and everything else identical. On the hard split, the
large test instances, \gadarbind{} reaches 4\% against a 6.00\% random floor
--- that is, it is indistinguishable from not having learned anything ---
while \gadar{} is at \todonum{7/7 solved when last observed; the sweep is
still in flight}. The step from 16\% to 100\% is produced by a component
that adds no learned parameters and no new kind of supervision: it only
states relations already present in the domain description in a form message
passing can use. This is the sense in which the domain description is data
rather than architecture --- and the sense in which the chain layer, not the
mere presence of the lifted domain in the graph, is what makes the
description actionable.

\textbf{The mechanism is not a terminal trigger.} An obvious deflationary
reading of a high easy-split number is that the policy has learned only to
fire when a goal atom is one action away, and that the domain's easy
instances forgive everything else. We measured that reading directly with the
achievement oracle described in the setup, which hard-codes exactly this
behavior with perfect knowledge: it bounds coverage at 50\% on easy Visitall
and at 0--5\% on Gripper and Miconic. \gadar{} exceeds that ceiling by a
factor of two on Visitall. Whatever the chain layer's goal distances,
chaining edges, and grounded effect edges give the network, it is more than a
terminal trigger; the policy is choosing actions that are several steps from
achieving anything, which is what the bucketed goal distance over the enables
relation was designed to support.

\textbf{Plan quality: near-planner-length plans, not lucky wandering.} On the
easy Visitall split the random floor is itself 99.73\%, because a monitored
random walk on a connected grid eventually visits every cell; coverage alone
therefore cannot distinguish competence from exhaustive wandering on this
split, and we said so in advance. Plan quality does distinguish them.
\gadar{}'s mean plan length is 30.1 steps against the satisficing planner's
29.2 on the same instances, a quality ratio of 0.97. A policy that solved
these instances by wandering under a 500-step bound would show a ratio an
order of magnitude worse. The zero-shot policy is, on this domain, producing
plans within 3\% of a planner that searches --- while itself performing no
search.

\textbf{Construction is essentially solved zero-shot.} V1, the fraction of
steps at which the top-ranked constructed action is applicable in the current
state, is 99.2\%. This is the metric that speaks to the capability claim in
its narrowest form: in a domain whose vocabulary the network has never seen,
the decoder assembles a schema and its arguments into an action the
environment accepts on essentially every step. The two failure modes are
cleanly separated by V1 and coverage together --- and on this domain neither
is currently binding.

\textbf{A methodological note: transfer peaks early.} Zero-shot performance
is not monotone in training progress, and the non-monotonicity is large. The
same run reaches 100.0\% coverage with V1 99.2\% at epoch 190, 97.6\%
coverage with V1 68.7\% at epoch 330, and degrades further by epoch 500,
while training loss decreases throughout. The same pattern, at smaller
magnitude, appears for \gadarbind{}: 16.0\% at epoch 260 falling to 3.3\% at
epoch 500. We read this as continued fitting of the training domains'
particulars at the expense of the domain-general structure --- an
overfitting phenomenon that in-domain validation loss does not see, because
in-domain validation is drawn from exactly the domains being overfitted to.
The consequence for protocol is that reporting only the final checkpoint
would understate every system in this table, and reporting a checkpoint
chosen on held-out-domain coverage would be selection on the test signal.
We therefore evaluate every saved checkpoint of every run and report the one
selected by combined training and validation loss --- a rule applied
identically to every system, in which the held-out domain plays no role. We
flag the sensitivity here because it is a property of cross-domain transfer
that any future work in this setting will encounter, and because it makes
the epoch at which a checkpoint is taken a reportable experimental detail
rather than an afterthought.

\section{Conclusion}

We introduced \gadar{}, a policy for classical planning that takes the domain
description as input and constructs grounded actions in domains never seen in
training, without search. The capability rests on a new representation ---
structural features, the lifted domain description as a graph, the
grounded--lifted binding layer connecting ground applicability to lifted
structure, and the chain layer that makes the domain's own producer--consumer
structure and its distance to the goal explicit --- while GABAR's encoder,
ranking decoder, and search-free execution carry over unchanged. The
representation is evaluated as it is built: a ladder of systems, each the
previous plus one commitment, so that every empirical gap certifies a
specific design decision. The binding layer is what makes ground
applicability expressible in the domain's own vocabulary, and Proposition~1
separates it from the lifted graphs of prior work; the chain layer is what
makes that structure reachable by message passing, and it is the gap that the
measurements isolate. On Visitall held out of training entirely, it moves
zero-shot coverage from 16\% to 100\% on training-scale instances at plan
quality 0.97 and 99.2\% top-1 proposal validity, above the ceiling of a
hand-coded goal-achievement oracle and, on the hard split, above a 6\% random
floor. We report this as one held-out domain measured end to end; the
remaining held-out targets, the union-vocabulary control, and the
training-set-diversity study are in flight, and every corresponding cell in
this paper is marked as a placeholder rather than filled in. Zero-shot
transfer is bounded by structure --- Proposition~2 makes the
ceiling precise --- and beyond that ceiling adaptation needs data from the
new domain: few-shot adaptation, lifted successor generation, richer PDDL
fragments, and probabilistic settings remain future work.
