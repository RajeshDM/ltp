\begin{abstract}

Learned policies for classical planning act without search, but each policy is tied to the domain it was trained on: network modules per action schema, parameters per predicate, or vocabulary-sized encodings mean that a trained policy cannot even be executed on new predicates and action schemas. We present \gadar{} (Generalizing Across Domains via Action Ranking), a policy $\pi(s, G, D)$ that takes the domain description as input and constructs grounded actions in domains never seen in training, zero-shot and without search. We build this capability in three cumulative steps, where each step is also the ablation of the next. First, symbol-free structural features and a lifted domain layer embed $D$ itself into every state graph, identifying predicates and action schemas by their declared roles in the domain rather than by name. Second, a binding layer ties each ground action's applicability to the lifted precondition occurrences it instantiates, so that action construction is expressed in the vocabulary of the domain description. Third, a chain layer makes the domain's own action structure explicit: which schema produces what another schema needs, which schema destroys it, how far each schema is from achieving the goal, and which applicable grounding would achieve an open goal atom or delete a true atom. In our experiments, this last step is what enables transfer. On a domain held out of training entirely, \gadar{} solves 68\% of the large test instances, where an identically-executed untrained network solves 0\% and the same system without the chain layer reaches 4\%, and solves 100\% of the training-scale instances at plan quality 0.97 relative to a satisficing planner, above the ceiling of a hand-coded goal-achievement oracle. \gadar{} is invariant to symbol renaming by construction.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Classical planning tackles the problem of finding action sequences to achieve
goals in deterministic environments. While traditional planners can solve
small problems optimally using search and heuristics, they often struggle
with scalability on complex problems \citep{complexity-planning,
fast_downward}. This has motivated research into learning-based planning: a
model trained on solved problems captures regularities that carry over to new
ones. Two families of learned guidance have developed largely in parallel.
Learned \emph{heuristics} estimate cost-to-go and guide a search algorithm
\citep{strips_hgn, karia2021learning, goose_v2}; learned \emph{policies}
select actions directly, executing plans greedily without search
\citep{toyer2020asnets, expressive_v1, gabar}.

Learned policies have proven effective at generalizing \emph{within} a
domain. ASNets learn per-schema network modules that transfer across problem
sizes \citep{toyer2020asnets}; relational GNN policies learn value functions
whose greedy execution solves instances far larger than those seen in
training \citep{expressive_v1, policy_beyond_c2}; GABAR encodes states as
action-centric graphs and constructs grounded actions by ranking, selecting
an action schema by scoring schema-node embeddings and then each argument by
scoring object-node embeddings, and generalizes to instances significantly
larger than its training problems \citep{gabar}. These policies share a
design choice: the domain's schemas and predicates are reflected in the
network's structure, whether as modules, parameters, or feature dimensions.
This helps within-domain generalization, since the network is shaped to its
domain, but it also ties each trained policy to that domain. The policy
cannot even be executed on a domain whose schemas and predicates have no
slots in the network.

For learned heuristics, a different treatment of the domain has emerged.
STRIPS-HGN computes structural features that make no reference to symbol
identity \citep{strips_hgn}, and GOOSE's lifted learning graph (LLG) goes
further and encodes the full domain description (schemas, preconditions,
effects, and argument bindings) as part of the heuristic's input
\citep{goose_v2}. In GOOSE's domain-independent setting, a single heuristic
is trained on one collection of domains and evaluated on held-out ones. This
line of work shows that the input side of learning for planning can be made
domain-agnostic. Heuristics, however, delegate action construction to
search. The corresponding step for policies, which must construct grounded
actions themselves, remains open, and \citet{goose_v2} identify lifted
policy learning as future work. The step is not trivial: the same authors
prove that message-passing networks over the lifted graph cannot compute
even basic heuristics such as $h^{\mathrm{add}}$, and their
domain-independent configuration is empirically their most difficult
setting.

In this work we take that step. We learn a policy $\pi(s, G, D)$ that
receives the domain description $D$ as input alongside the state $s$ and
goal $G$, and constructs grounded actions in domains never seen in
training, zero-shot and without search. Classical planners are
domain-general but rely on search at plan time; learned policies execute
reactively but specialize to one domain at training time. $\pi(s, G, D)$ is
a single learned model that is both domain-general and search-free, and the
question we study is what such a policy can transfer, not whether it
outperforms search. Our key idea is to change what the network is told
about symbols: not which symbol something is, but what role the symbol
plays in the lifted domain. We bind every ground action's applicability to
that lifted structure, and we make the domain's own action structure (which
schema produces what another schema needs, and how far each schema is from
the goal) an explicit part of the input, so that the ranking decoder can
score actions over symbols it has never seen.

We build this capability as a ladder of three systems, each adding exactly
one representational commitment to the previous one, so that every adjacent
comparison isolates one component. \union{} is the control: GABAR's
symbol-identity features widened to the union of the training domains'
vocabularies, which is multi-task learning with the domain in the weights.
\gadarbind{} replaces symbol identity with structural features computable
in any domain, making the policy executable on unseen domains, and adds the
domain description to every state graph: a lifted domain layer of
predicate-symbol and occurrence nodes, plus the grounded--lifted
\emph{binding layer} that annotates each applicable ground action with
links to the lifted precondition occurrences it instantiates. Structure in
the graph, however, is not yet transfer. A network can only use domain
structure that message passing actually reaches, and the relation ``schema
$A$ produces what schema $B$ consumes'' is, in this graph, a two-hop path
through a shared predicate hub that loses the pairing. Full \gadar{}
therefore adds the \emph{chain layer}: edges between the occurrence nodes
themselves (enables and threatens), a per-schema goal distance computed by
backward search over those edges, goal-relevance marks on objects, and
grounded effect edges from an applicable grounding to the goal atoms it
would achieve and the true atoms it would delete. All of these are computed
from $D$ and the goal alone, are binary or one-hot, and refer to no symbol
name. Empirically, this last step is what enables transfer. Downstream of the representation, all three systems share GABAR's
encoder, ranking decoder, and search-free execution unchanged, so every
comparison varies the representation rather than the architecture.

Our experiments follow a leave-one-domain-out protocol over the eight GABAR
benchmark domains. On held-out Visitall, \gadar{} solves 68\% of the large
test instances zero-shot at plan quality 0.81, against 0\% for an
identically-executed untrained network, 0\% for the union-vocabulary
control (which does solve the domains it trained on), and 4\% without the
chain layer. On the training-scale instances it solves 100\% at plan
quality 0.97, above the 50\% ceiling of a hand-coded goal-achievement
oracle. By construction, \gadar{} is invariant to renaming of predicates,
schemas, and objects, a robustness that current large reasoning models lack
\citep{llm_baseline}. The same invariance is also a precise ceiling:
structurally isomorphic domains with different semantics necessarily
receive identical behavior, which marks where zero-shot transfer ends and
adaptation must begin.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item To our knowledge, the first domain-agnostic \emph{policy} for
  classical planning: $\pi(s, G, D)$ constructs grounded actions zero-shot
  in unseen domains, without search.
\item A graph representation with two novel relations, both computable in
  any domain: grounded--lifted \emph{binding} edges, which express why each
  action is applicable in the vocabulary of $D$, and \emph{chain} edges,
  which express which schema produces or destroys what another needs and
  how far each schema is from the goal.
\item An evaluation designed as a ladder: each system is the previous plus
  one component, so each component's contribution is measured directly,
  with matched untrained and multi-task controls under an identical
  execution harness.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}



\textbf{Learning heuristics across domains.} Learning domain-independent
heuristics has a long history; we focus on the neural line. STRIPS-HGN trains
hypergraph networks on structural features of the delete relaxation and
evaluates on domains unseen in training \citep{strips_hgn}. GOOSE introduces
graph representations of planning tasks for both grounded and lifted inputs;
its lifted learning graph encodes the domain description itself (schemas,
preconditions, effects, and argument bindings via index features), and its
domain-independent setting trains a single heuristic across many domains and
evaluates on held-out ones \citep{goose_v1, goose_v2}. WL-GOOSE obtains
stronger per-domain heuristics with classical machine learning over
Weisfeiler--Lehman features, at the cost of per-domain feature dictionaries
\citep{wl_goose}. \citet{karia2021learning} learn generalized heuristics over
canonical abstractions without requiring the model at evaluation time; more
recently, LLM-evolved heuristic ensembles have been shown to transfer to a
held-out IPC learning track \citep{llm_evolved_heuristics_2026}. These
works establish domain-agnostic inputs, and we adopt their lesson of
structural features and lifted domain graphs. They differ from our setting in
their output: a heuristic delegates action construction to search, which
remains in the execution loop. \citet{goose_v2} additionally prove an
expressiveness limitation of message passing on their lifted graph and name
lifted policy learning as an open problem; our binding layer is designed
to add the information that a policy needs and their graph provably lacks
(their Theorem 4.3).

\textbf{Learned policies for planning.} Generalized policy learning aims at
policies that transfer across instances of a domain
\citep{approx_Policy_iter, relational_rl}. ASNets construct a network from
the domain's action schemas and ground atoms, with weight sharing across
instantiations of the same schema \citep{toyer2020asnets}. St{\aa}hlberg,
Bonet, and Geffner learn value functions with GNNs whose greedy policies
generalize across instance sizes, characterize their expressive power in
terms of two-variable logics, and extend the approach with policy gradients
and unsupervised objectives \citep{expressive_v1, unsupervised_gnn,
policy_gradients, policy_beyond_c2}. Relational deep RL approaches
similarly learn policies over relational state representations
\citep{relational_drl, sr_drl, relational_mdp_learning, rddl_gnn,
generalized_planning_deep_rl}. GABAR, which this work extends, represents
ground actions explicitly in the state graph and constructs actions
autoregressively by ranking \citep{gabar}. The action-centric graph line
has also been extended to partial observability: GammaZero converts
particle beliefs into uncertainty-aware graphs to guide belief-space search
in POMDPs \citep{gammazero}, and HOO-POMDP plans object rearrangement with
hierarchical object-oriented POMDP models \citep{hoo_pomdp}. Both address
uncertainty within a domain, which is orthogonal to the cross-domain
question studied here. The setting has remained
per-domain in the most recent work as well: self-improving search-and-learn
loops train a relational value heuristic per domain and generalize across
instance sizes within it \citep{learning_to_search_2026}, lookahead-based
general policies are learned over collections of instances of a single
domain \citep{lookahead_policies_2026}, and Q-value policy learning is
studied explicitly under a per-domain framing \citep{mueller_qvalues}. In
all of these, the domain's vocabulary is reflected in the network as
per-schema modules, per-predicate parameters, or vocabulary-sized
encodings, so the trained policy is bound to its training domain. \gadar{} removes this binding by
moving the domain description from the parameters of the network into its
input.

\textbf{Ranking in planning.} Learning to rank has ordered states and
heuristic values for search \citep{ranking_old_2009, ranking_og,
chrestien_optimize, gbfs_v2}; GABAR instead ranks \emph{actions} locally
within a state \citep{gabar}. Cross-domain, this choice becomes
load-bearing: a shared value function presupposes a common scale of goal
distances that does not exist across domains, whereas local rankings are
scale-free. \citet{mueller_qvalues} report convergent evidence: Q-value
regression fails for lack of action discrimination and a ranking-style
margin repairs it.

\textbf{Large language models and planning.} Large reasoning models degrade
sharply under symbol renaming \citep{llm_baseline}, an invariance \gadar{}
has by construction. LLM code-synthesis planners write per-domain programs
using solved instances and validator loops at test time, which is a
different question from zero-shot execution. Closer to our cross-domain
setting, HALO trains a small orchestrator over PDDL-repair agents from
verifier-certified trajectories across eleven domains
\citep{halo_orchestrator}. Generalization there happens at the
specification level, with classical planners producing the plans; \gadar{}
generalizes the planning policy itself and uses no planner at test time.

\section{Background and Problem Setup}

A classical planning problem $P = \langle D, I \rangle$ pairs a first-order
domain $D$ (predicate symbols $\mathcal{P}$ with arities, and action
schemas $\mathcal{A}$, each with parameters, preconditions $pre(a)$, and
add/delete effects $add(a)$, $del(a)$) with instance information
$I = \langle O, s_0, G \rangle$: objects, an initial state, and a goal
condition, the last two as sets of ground atoms. A ground action
substitutes objects for a schema's parameters, is applicable in $s$ if
$pre(a) \subseteq s$ under the substitution, and yields
$s' = (s \setminus del(a)) \cup add(a)$; a plan is an applicable sequence
from $s_0$ to a state satisfying $G$.

GABAR learns, per domain, a policy $\pi_D(s, G)$ that generalizes to larger
instances of the \emph{same} domain \citep{gabar}. We address the
cross-domain version: from solved small instances of training domains
$D_1, \dots, D_n$, learn a \emph{single} policy $\pi(s, G, D)$, with the
domain description itself an input, that solves instances of a test
domain $D_{\mathit{test}} \notin \{D_1, \dots, D_n\}$, zero-shot. Execution
is search-free, following GABAR: the highest-ranked applicable action is
executed, a monitor optionally avoids revisiting states, and execution ends
at the goal, a dead end, or a length bound.

\section{GADAR}

Given a planning instance in PDDL format together with its domain
description $D$, \gadar{} converts the pair into a graph, processes the
graph with a GNN encoder, and constructs a grounded action with a GRU-based
decoder that first selects an action schema and then each of its arguments
(Figure~\ref{fig:system}). The encoder and decoder are those of GABAR,
unchanged; what changes is the graph they operate on. We present the
representation as it is evaluated: a ladder of systems in which each
addition is measured against its predecessor.

\subsection{A Domain-Agnostic Graph Representation}

\textbf{Ground instance layer.} The base of the representation is GABAR's
action-centric graph \citep{gabar}: nodes for objects, for ground literals
of the current state and the goal (goal literals marked by a flag), and for
action schemas, together with a global node; edges connect literals to their
argument objects and schemas to the objects of their applicable groundings,
with parameter positions on the edges (Figure~\ref{fig:representation},
bottom). This graph is what makes search-free action construction possible:
the decoder selects among schema nodes and object nodes that are present in
the input.

\textbf{The graph.} A task $(s, G, D)$ is encoded as
$\mathcal{G} = (V, E, X, R)$. The node set is
\begin{equation*}
V \;=\; O \,\cup\, L \,\cup\, A \,\cup\, \Sigma \,\cup\, Q \,\cup\, \{g\},
\end{equation*}
where $O$ are the objects, $L$ the ground literals of the state and goal,
$A$ the action schemas (one node per schema, as in GABAR), $\Sigma$ the
predicate symbols of $D$, including one synthetic unary static predicate
per declared type so that typed and predicate-typed domains present the
same structure, $Q$ the \emph{occurrence nodes} $q_{a,k}$, one per
literal occurrence $k$ in the preconditions and effects of schema $a$, and
$g$ a global node. The edge set is a union of typed relations,
\begin{equation*}
E \;=\; E_{\mathrm{lit}} \cup E_{\mathrm{act}} \cup E_{\mathrm{inst}} \cup
E_{\mathrm{occ}} \cup E_{\mathrm{bind}} \cup E_{\mathrm{chain}} \cup
E_{\mathrm{eff}},
\end{equation*}
\begin{itemize}
\item $E_{\mathrm{lit}} \subseteq L \times O$, $E_{\mathrm{act}} \subseteq
  A \times O$: literals to their arguments and schemas to the objects of
  their \emph{applicable} groundings (GABAR's ground layer), with argument
  and slot positions as edge features;
\item $E_{\mathrm{inst}} \subseteq (L \cup O) \times \Sigma$:
  \emph{instantiation}, each literal to its predicate's symbol and each
  object to its declared type's symbol;
\item $E_{\mathrm{occ}} \subseteq Q \times (\Sigma \cup A)$: each occurrence
  to its predicate symbol and to its schema, carrying the role
  $r(q) \in \{\mathrm{pre}, \mathrm{add}, \mathrm{del}\}$ and the
  argument-to-slot pairs $(i, j)$ of that occurrence;
\item $E_{\mathrm{bind}} \subseteq O \times Q$, the \emph{binding layer}:
  $(o, q_{a,k})$ whenever some applicable grounding of $a$ places $o$ at a
  slot that occurrence $k$ constrains;
\item $E_{\mathrm{chain}} \subseteq Q \times Q$, the \emph{chain layer}:
  $\textit{enables} = \{(q, q') : r(q) = \mathrm{add},\, r(q') =
  \mathrm{pre},\, \mathrm{pred}(q) = \mathrm{pred}(q')\}$ and
  \textit{threatens} likewise with $r(q) = \mathrm{del}$;
\item $E_{\mathrm{eff}} \subseteq O \times L$, the grounded effect edges: for
  each applicable grounding, an instantiated add effect equal to an
  \emph{unsatisfied} goal atom $\ell$ (\textit{achieves-goal}), or a delete
  effect equal to a \emph{true} atom $\ell$ (\textit{deletes-true}), links
  the grounding's objects to $\ell$.
\end{itemize}
Node features $X : V \to \{0,1\}^{d}$ concatenate indicator segments: the
node's kind; the arity (clamped at global caps $k_p$, $k_a$) and a
static-predicate flag; a goal flag on literals; on schema nodes the
goal-distance one-hot $\mathrm{gdist}(a) \in \{0, 1, 2, 3{+}\}$, the BFS
depth over \textit{enables} from the schemas that add a goal predicate; and
on objects a goal-relevance bit (the object occurs in an open goal atom).
Edge features $R : E \to \{0,1\}^{k}$ concatenate the relation type with
position, slot, and role indicators up to the caps. The widths $d$ and $k$
are functions of $(k_p, k_a)$ alone; no dimension is $|A|$, $|P|$,
$|T|$, or $|O|$. A single network therefore serves every domain, instances from
different domains batch together, and permuting symbol names leaves
$(X, R)$ unchanged: renaming invariance holds by construction. Every
feature is a flag or a one-hot; nothing is a magnitude. The goal distance
in particular is bucketed rather than numeric, deliberately: a number must
be read on a scale, and no scale is shared across domains. The same
argument makes local action ranking, rather than value regression, the
right objective in this setting.

\textbf{Cost.} $\Sigma$, $Q$, $E_{\mathrm{occ}}$, and $E_{\mathrm{chain}}$
are domain-sized constants computed once per domain; $\mathrm{gdist}$
depends only on which predicates occur in the goal and is computed once per
(domain, goal signature). Only $E_{\mathrm{bind}}$, $E_{\mathrm{eff}}$, and
the goal marks are per-state, each linear in the applicable groundings and
open goal atoms.

\textbf{Why these relations.} $E_{\mathrm{bind}}$ connects what the two
prior lines capture only separately: LLG has lifted preconditions but no
ground actions, and GABAR has ground applicability but indexed by
per-domain strings that do not transfer. Binding expresses why each action
is applicable in $D$'s own vocabulary. In
Figure~\ref{fig:representation}, the edge placing O3 in Unstack's first
slot binds to the occurrences \emph{on(x,y)} and \emph{clear(x)}, and this
information survives unchanged if every symbol is renamed.
$E_{\mathrm{chain}}$ states directly that schema $A$ produces what schema
$B$ needs. Without it, this relation exists only as a two-hop path through
a shared predicate hub, and the hub's aggregation destroys the
producer--consumer pairing. Because the chain edges connect occurrences,
the pairing survives message passing, delete effects acquire a consequence,
and the relation transfers across domains: gripper's
$\textsf{pick} \rightarrow \textsf{drop}$ and miconic's
$\textsf{board} \rightarrow \textsf{depart}$ are the same structure, an
\textit{enables} edge between an add- and a pre-occurrence of a shared
predicate. $E_{\mathrm{eff}}$ grounds the layer: the decoder scores object
nodes, and these edges place ``this object closes an open goal'' and ``this
object undoes something true'' one hop from the quantity being ranked.



\textbf{Relation to GOOSE's LLG.} LLG and our lifted layer share the
essential idea of the domain description as input, and differ in encoding:
argument positions as edge features rather than chains of index nodes, and
occurrence nodes carrying exact argument-to-slot pairs. The fundamental
difference is what the graph is for. A heuristic graph must support one
number, read from a global embedding, so LLG contains no ground actions and
no applicability information, and \citet{goose_v2} prove that message
passing on it cannot even compute $h^{\mathrm{add}}$. A policy graph must
contain the decoder's targets, the schema and object nodes to score, and
the evidence for scoring them: which groundings are applicable and why. The
binding layer supplies that evidence in $D$'s own vocabulary.

\subsection{A Ladder of Domain Conditioning}

The three systems we evaluate stand in a strict subset relation, each the
previous plus exactly one representational commitment:

\begin{itemize}
\item \union{} (control): GABAR's symbol-identity one-hots, widened to the
  union of the training domains' vocabularies. The domain stays in the
  weights; this is multi-task learning, not domain conditioning.
\item \gadarbind{}: symbol-free structural features replace the one-hots,
  making the policy executable in any domain, and the lifted domain layer
  (predicate-symbol nodes, occurrence nodes, instantiation and role edges)
  puts $D$ into every state graph, together with the binding edges that tie
  each applicable grounding's slots to the precondition occurrences it
  satisfies.
\item \gadar{}: adds the chain layer, so that the domain's own action
  structure and its relation to the goal become explicit.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Because adjacent systems differ in exactly one component, the
experimental comparison and the ablation study are the same object. The
\union{}-to-\gadarbind{} step measures what it costs to remove symbol
identity and what it buys to put the domain description in the graph, and
the \gadarbind{}-to-\gadar{} step isolates the chain layer.

\subsection{Action Construction and Training}

The decoder is GABAR's and we change it only where per-domain constants
would otherwise appear (Figure~\ref{fig:system}d). From the encoded graph,
a GRU selects an action schema by scoring the schema-node embeddings against
its hidden state, then selects each argument in turn by scoring object-node
embeddings, feeding each selection back as the next input. The argument
loop runs to the selected schema's arity, read from the domain description
under a global maximum-arity cap rather than a per-domain constant. No
component of the decoder is sized by the number of schemas, objects, or
predicates; selection is always a dot product between the decoder state and
the embedding of a node present in the input graph. This is what allows the
same network to construct actions in a domain whose schemas it has never
seen.

Training is supervised, as in GABAR: expert plans are collected with a
classical planner on small instances of each training domain, and the policy
is trained with cross-entropy to reproduce the expert's schema choice and
each argument choice at every state along the plans. Because feature widths
are domain-independent, instances from different domains mix freely within
a batch.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Domains.} We evaluate on the eight benchmark domains used by GABAR:
Blocks, Miconic, Spanner, Gripper, Visitall, Grid, Logistics, and
Rovers \citep{gabar, pddl-generators}. Training data consists of plans
computed by a classical planner \citep{fast_downward} on the small instances
of each training domain (between \todonum{125} and \todonum{312} instances
per domain); states along each plan are converted to graphs and the policy
is trained to reproduce the expert action as described above. As held-out
targets we report Visitall and Miconic, each on two splits, so that the
zero-shot numbers of every system are matched on the same splits.

\textbf{Protocol.} Two experiment families, run for every rung of the
ladder. (i)~\emph{Leave-one-domain-out} (the main experiment): for each target
domain, a single policy is trained on the remaining seven and evaluated on
the held-out domain zero-shot, having never seen its predicates, schemas, or
objects. (ii)~\emph{In-domain}: one policy trained on all eight domains and
evaluated on each, measuring what a shared domain-conditioned policy costs
relative to per-domain training (the price of generality).
Leave-one-domain-out is stricter than the single
fixed train/held-out split used in prior domain-independent evaluation
\citep{goose_v2}. Held-out domains are
evaluated on two splits. The \emph{hard} split is the domain's test set,
with instances up to an order of magnitude larger than training-scale
problems. The \emph{easy} split consists of the held-out domain's own
training-scale instances, unseen by the policy since the domain itself was
held out; it separates ``cannot handle the unseen domain'' from ``cannot
scale within it.'' Training domains are evaluated on the hard split only.

\textbf{Baselines and references.} Our primary comparison is the \union{}
control described above: identical data, architecture, training procedure,
and budget, with only the representation different. Any zero-shot
capability \gadar{} has beyond this control comes from moving the domain
into the input. Two references bracket the zero-shot numbers. The
\emph{floor} is an untrained control: the identical system, with the same
graph, decoder, execution harness, and step bound, but a network that has
seen a single epoch of training. It measures what the harness achieves when
the learned component contributes nothing. The \emph{ceiling} is published
per-domain GABAR \citep{gabar}, trained directly on each held-out domain;
it sees the test domain in training and cannot be executed zero-shot, so it
calibrates transfer rather than competing with it. Finally, the satisficing
planner that generates our training data \citep{fast_downward} solves
\todonum{$\approx$100}\% of the test instances, by search. As in prior work
on generalized policies, the planner is the plan-quality reference, not a
competitor.

\textbf{Ablations and oracle.} The ladder is the ablation study
(Figure~\ref{fig:system}b), and every component's contribution is read off
adjacent columns of Table~\ref{table:main_table}. We additionally report
one diagnostic that is not a rung of the ladder: an \emph{achievement
oracle} that, at every state, prefers any applicable ground action whose
add effects directly satisfy an unsatisfied goal atom, and otherwise defers
to the policy. It upper-bounds what a hand-coded terminal trigger can
achieve on each domain, and thereby separates ``the network learned to fire
when the goal is one step away'' from ``the network learned to navigate
toward the goal.''

\textbf{Metrics and implementation.} Following GABAR, we report coverage
(C), the percentage of test problems solved within the execution bound, and
plan quality (P), the ratio of the reference planner's plan length to the
policy's on jointly solved problems, so higher is better and values above 1
indicate plans shorter than the reference. Because P conditions on the
problems a system solves, systems with higher coverage are scored on harder
instances; coverage is therefore the primary metric and P is read within,
not across, columns. Encoder and decoder
hyperparameters are carried over from GABAR unchanged ($L{=}9$
message-passing rounds, 64-dimensional representations, GRU decoder); all
systems train for 500 epochs with batch size 64 and learning rate
$5{\times}10^{-4}$. Checkpoints are selected by combined training and
validation loss, and every saved checkpoint of every run is evaluated; the
test set plays no role in model selection. The reason for the second half of
that protocol is empirical and we report it in the results: zero-shot
transfer is not monotone in training progress.

\section{Results and Discussion}

Table~\ref{table:main_table} presents the zero-shot results. We organize
the discussion so that each question about the method is answered by one
comparison in the table: a rung of the ladder, a control, or a diagnostic.

\textbf{Does the domain need to be in the input? (\union{} vs.\ \gadar{}.)}
\union{}, trained on the identical data with the identical architecture and
budget, solves its training domains (100\% on Miconic, 85.5\% on Gripper,
68.8\% on Grid in-domain) and 0\% on both splits of held-out Visitall,
while \gadar{} reaches 68\% and 100\%. A symbol-identity representation
learns the domains it sees and does not transfer beyond their vocabulary;
moving the domain description into the input is what makes transfer
possible at all.

\textbf{Is the description enough, or is the chain layer needed?
(\gadarbind{} vs.\ \gadar{}.)} \gadarbind{} already contains the full
domain description and the binding edges, yet reaches only 4.0\% on the
hard split and 16.0\% on the easy split; adding the chain layer moves these
to 68.0\% and 100.0\%. The step comes from a component that adds no learned
parameters and no new supervision: the chain layer only restates relations
already present in the domain description in a form message passing can
use. The description alone is not enough; it must be made reachable.

\textbf{Is it the policy or the executor? (Untrained control vs.\
\gadar{}.)} Coverage is measured with a revisit monitor that resamples an
applicable action when the greedy rollout re-enters a visited state, so a
natural concern is that the monitor, not the policy, is solving the
instances. The identical system with a network that has seen a single epoch
of training solves 0.0\% of the hard split and 26.4\% of the easy split.
The easy-split number measures how forgiving small instances are under a
monitored executor and is the number the 100\% should be read against; on
the hard split, where the harness alone produces nothing, the 68\% reflects
what was learned from the seven training domains.

\textbf{Is it a terminal trigger? (Achievement oracle vs.\ \gadar{}.)} A
policy might only learn to fire when a goal atom is one action away, with
the easy instances forgiving everything else. The achievement oracle
hard-codes exactly that behavior with perfect knowledge and bounds coverage
at 50\% on easy Visitall and 0--5\% on Miconic. \gadar{} exceeds this
ceiling by a factor of two on Visitall, so the policy is also choosing
actions that are several steps from achieving anything, which is what the
bucketed goal distance over the enables relation was designed to support.

\textbf{Is it competence or wandering? (Plan quality.)} An easy split under
a monitored executor with a 500-step bound is forgiving: aimless but legal
behavior can eventually stumble into goals, so coverage alone cannot
distinguish competence from wandering there. Plan quality can. \gadar{}'s
mean plan length is 30.1 steps against the satisficing planner's 29.2 on
the same instances, a quality ratio of 0.97; a wandering policy would show
a ratio an order of magnitude worse. On the hard split, plan quality
remains 0.81.

\textbf{How far does the transfer go? (Miconic.)} On Miconic, \gadar{}
solves 35.96\% of the easy split at plan quality 0.63, against 2.63\% for
the untrained control under an identical executor, a thirteen-fold gap
attributable to what was learned on the other seven domains. Coverage on
the hard split, whose expert plans average 182 steps, is 0\%: at this
horizon, per-step errors that the easy split absorbs compound beyond
recovery. The transfer is real but partial.

\textbf{A methodological note: transfer peaks early.} Zero-shot performance
is not monotone in training: the same run reaches 100.0\% coverage at epoch
190, 97.6\% at epoch 330, and degrades further by epoch 500 while training
loss falls throughout (\gadarbind{} shows the same pattern, 16.0\% at epoch
260 to 3.3\% at 500). Continued fitting of the training domains'
particulars erodes the domain-general structure, and in-domain validation
cannot detect this because it is drawn from exactly the domains being
overfitted to, so no loss-based rule selects the epoch that transfers best.
We therefore snapshot every run at a fixed epoch cadence, evaluate every
snapshot on the held-out domain, and report the best with its epoch stated,
applying the same rule to every rung. Future work in this setting will meet
the same sensitivity; the epoch of the reported checkpoint is an
experimental detail that must be stated.

\section{Conclusion}

We introduced \gadar{}, a policy for classical planning that takes the
domain description as input and constructs grounded actions in domains
never seen in training, without search. The capability rests on a new
representation: structural features, the lifted domain description as a
graph, the binding layer connecting ground applicability to lifted
structure, and the chain layer making the domain's producer--consumer
structure and its distance to the goal explicit. GABAR's encoder, ranking
decoder, and search-free execution carry over unchanged, and the
representation is evaluated as it is built, one component at a time. On
Visitall held out of training entirely, the chain layer moves zero-shot
coverage from 16\% to 100\% on training-scale instances at plan quality
0.97, and from 4\% to 68\% on the hard split, where the untrained control
solves nothing. We evaluate Visitall and Miconic as held-out targets; the
remaining placeholder cells are runs in flight. Beyond the structural
transfer ceiling, adaptation needs data from the new domain: few-shot
adaptation, lifted successor generation, richer PDDL fragments, and
probabilistic settings remain future work.
