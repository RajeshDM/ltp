\begin{abstract}
Learned policies for classical planning act without search, but each policy is
tied to the domain it was trained on: network modules per action schema,
parameters per predicate, or vocabulary-sized encodings mean a trained policy
cannot be executed on new predicates and action schemas. We propose \gadar{}
(Generalizing Across Domains via Action Ranking), the first policy for
classical planning that takes the domain description as input --- $\pi(s, G,
D)$ --- and constructs grounded actions in domains never seen in training,
zero-shot and without search. The capability comes from a new representation:
symbol-free structural features, the lifted domain description embedded as a
graph, and a \emph{binding layer} that expresses each ground action's
applicability in the vocabulary of the lifted domain. We instantiate domain
conditioning in two ways: \gadarc{} compiles $D$ once per domain into static
symbol embeddings, while \gadarj{} joins $D$ into every state graph, computing
symbol meaning in context --- a comparison that tests whether a domain's
symbols can mean the same thing in every state. Trained on \todonum{$N-1$}
standard domains, both methods solve \todonum{X}\% of tasks in held-out
domains where a union-vocabulary multi-task control solves \todonum{Y}\%, and
removing the binding layer collapses transfer to \todonum{Z}\%. \gadar{} is
invariant to symbol renaming by construction, and we prove the binding layer
strictly increases expressiveness over the lifted graphs used for
cross-domain heuristic learning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Classical planning tackles the problem of finding action sequences to achieve
goals in deterministic environments. While traditional planners can solve
small problems optimally using search and heuristics, they often struggle
with scalability on complex problems \citep{complexity-planning,
fast_downward}. This has motivated research into learning-based planning: a
model trained on solved problems captures regularities that carry over to new
ones. Two families of learned guidance have developed largely in parallel.
Learned \emph{heuristics} estimate cost-to-go and guide a search algorithm
\citep{strips_hgn, karia2021learning, goose_v2}; learned \emph{policies}
select actions directly, executing plans greedily without search
\citep{toyer2020asnets, expressive_v1, gabar}.

Learned policies have proven effective at generalizing \emph{within} a
domain. ASNets learn per-schema network modules that transfer across problem
sizes \citep{toyer2020asnets}; relational GNN policies learn value functions
whose greedy execution solves instances far larger than those seen in
training \citep{expressive_v1, policy_beyond_c2}; GABAR encodes states as
action-centric graphs and constructs grounded actions by ranking ---
selecting an action schema by scoring schema-node embeddings, then each
argument by scoring object-node embeddings --- generalizing to instances
significantly larger than its training problems \citep{gabar}. These policies
share a design choice: the domain's schemas and predicates are reflected in
the network's structure, whether as modules, parameters, or feature
dimensions. This is a strength for within-domain generalization --- the
network is shaped to its domain --- but it ties each trained policy to the
domain it was trained on: the policy cannot be executed on a domain whose
schemas and predicates have no slots in the network.

For learned heuristics, a different treatment of the domain has emerged.
STRIPS-HGN computes structural features that make no reference to symbol
identity \citep{strips_hgn}, and GOOSE's lifted learning graph (LLG) goes
further, encoding the full domain description --- schemas, preconditions,
effects, argument bindings --- as part of the heuristic's input
\citep{goose_v2}. In GOOSE's domain-independent setting, a single heuristic
is trained on one collection of domains and evaluated on held-out ones. This
line of work establishes that the \emph{input} side of learning for planning
can be made domain-agnostic. Heuristics, however, delegate action
construction to search, and the corresponding step for policies --- which
must construct grounded actions themselves --- remains open; \citet{goose_v2}
identify lifted policy learning as future work. It is a natural next step,
and a nontrivial one: the same authors prove that message-passing networks
over the lifted graph cannot compute even basic heuristics such as
$h^{\mathrm{add}}$, and their domain-independent configuration is empirically
their most difficult setting.

In this work we take that step. We learn a policy $\pi(s, G, D)$ that
receives the domain description $D$ as input alongside the state $s$ and goal
$G$, and constructs grounded actions in domains never seen in training ---
zero-shot, without search. Our key idea is to change what the network is told
about symbols: not \emph{which symbol} something is, but \emph{what role the
symbol plays} in the lifted domain --- and to bind every ground action's
applicability to that lifted structure, so that the ranking decoder can score
actions over symbols it has never seen. \gadar{}'s representation combines
three elements: (1) structural features computable for any domain, in place
of symbol-identity encodings; (2) the lifted domain description embedded as a
graph, in the spirit of GOOSE; and (3) the element we introduce, a
\emph{grounded--lifted binding layer} that annotates each applicable ground
action with links to the lifted precondition occurrences it instantiates. The
binding layer connects what the two prior lines each capture only partially
--- lifted domain structure on one side, ground applicability on the other
--- and we prove the combination is strictly more expressive than the lifted
graph alone: message-passing networks on the binding-layer graph distinguish
planning tasks whose optimal actions differ but that are indistinguishable on
LLG (Proposition~1).

We instantiate domain conditioning in two ways, corresponding to two
hypotheses about what a domain's symbols are. \gadarc{} (\emph{compiled})
encodes the domain once and injects static symbol embeddings into every state
graph; per-state inference cost matches per-domain GABAR. \gadarj{}
(\emph{joint}) includes the lifted domain graph in every state graph, so that
symbol meaning is contextualized by message passing over the current state
and goal. The comparison is informative in either direction: an advantage for
\gadarj{} --- particularly on structurally similar domains, where the same
role is played by symbols with different semantics --- indicates that symbol
meaning must be computed in context, while parity means domain conditioning
can be amortized per domain. Downstream of the representation, \gadar{}
retains GABAR's encoder, ranking decoder, and search-free execution
unchanged, so every comparison in our experiments varies the representation
rather than the architecture. The ranking objective, one design choice among
several in the single-domain setting, does additional work here: rankings are
scale-free across domains, whereas value functions presuppose a scale shared
between domains --- a point we examine by ablation and that aligns with
observations for learned heuristics \citep{chrestien_optimize, goose_v2}.

Through experiments on \todonum{the eight GABAR benchmark domains} in a
leave-one-domain-out protocol, we show that: (a) zero-shot on held-out
domains, \gadar{} solves \todonum{X}\% of tasks where a union-vocabulary
multi-task control --- sharing \gadar{}'s data, architecture, and training,
but keeping the domain in its weights --- solves \todonum{Y}\%; (b) removing
the binding layer while keeping the remainder of the representation reduces
coverage to \todonum{Z}\%, confirming it as the enabling component; and (c)
\todonum{finding of the compiled-vs-joint comparison}. On training domains,
structural features cost \todonum{$\cdot$} relative to symbol-identity
features, which we report as the price of generality. By construction,
\gadar{} is invariant to renaming of predicates, schemas, and objects --- a
robustness that symbol-based approaches, including current large reasoning
models, do not share \citep{llm_baseline}

--- and the same invariance yields a precise transfer ceiling: structurally
isomorphic domains with different semantics necessarily receive identical
behavior (Proposition~2). Beyond that ceiling, transfer requires data, and
few-shot adaptation curves show \gadar{} reaches per-domain GABAR's coverage
with \todonum{$\cdot$}\% of its training data.

\textbf{Contribution.} We present the first domain-agnostic policy for
classical planning: zero-shot grounded-action construction in unseen domains,
without search, achieved through a domain-as-graph representation whose key
component --- the grounded--lifted binding layer --- we isolate by ablation
and justify by a strict expressiveness separation, and realized in two
conditioning methods, compiled and joint, whose comparison answers whether
domain semantics can be compiled once or must be computed in context.

\textbf{Learning heuristics across domains.} Learning domain-independent
heuristics has a long history; we focus on the neural line. STRIPS-HGN trains
hypergraph networks on structural features of the delete relaxation and
evaluates on domains unseen in training \citep{strips_hgn}. GOOSE introduces
graph representations of planning tasks for both grounded and lifted inputs;
its lifted learning graph encodes the domain description itself --- schemas,
preconditions, effects, and argument bindings via index features --- and its
domain-independent setting trains a single heuristic across many domains and
evaluates on held-out ones \citep{goose_v1, goose_v2}. WL-GOOSE obtains
stronger per-domain heuristics with classical machine learning over
Weisfeiler--Lehman features, at the cost of per-domain feature dictionaries
\citep{wl_goose}. \citet{karia2021learning} learn generalized heuristics over
canonical abstractions without requiring the model at evaluation time. These
works establish domain-agnostic \emph{inputs}, and we adopt their lesson ---
structural features and lifted domain graphs. They differ from our setting in
their output: a heuristic delegates action construction to search, which
remains in the execution loop. \citet{goose_v2} additionally prove an
expressiveness limitation of message passing on their lifted graph and name
lifted policy learning as an open problem; our binding layer is designed
precisely to add the information that a policy needs and their graph
provably lacks (Proposition~1).

\textbf{Learned policies for planning.} Generalized policy learning aims at
policies that transfer across instances of a domain
\citep{approx_Policy_iter, relational_rl}. ASNets construct a network from
the domain's action schemas and ground atoms, with weight sharing across
instantiations of the same schema \citep{toyer2020asnets}. St{\aa}hlberg,
Bonet, and Geffner learn value functions with GNNs whose greedy policies
generalize across instance sizes, characterize their expressive power in
terms of two-variable logics, and extend the approach with policy gradients
and unsupervised objectives \citep{expressive_v1, unsupervised_gnn,
policy_gradients, policy_beyond_c2}. Relational deep RL approaches
similarly learn policies over relational state representations
\citep{relational_drl, sr_drl, relational_mdp_learning, rddl_gnn,
generalized_planning_deep_rl}. GABAR, which this work extends, represents
ground actions explicitly in the state graph and constructs actions
autoregressively by ranking \citep{gabar}. In all of these, the domain's
vocabulary is reflected in the network --- as per-schema modules,
per-predicate parameters, or vocabulary-sized encodings --- so the trained
policy is bound to its training domain. \gadar{} removes this binding by
moving the domain description from the parameters of the network into its
input.

\textbf{Ranking in planning.} Learning to rank has been used to order
states and heuristic values for search: linear ranking functions for beam
search \citep{ranking_old_2009}, RankSVM-based heuristic synthesis
\citep{ranking_og}, ranking-optimized heuristics \citep{chrestien_optimize},
and learned pairwise rankings for greedy best-first search \citep{gbfs_v2}.
GABAR instead ranks \emph{actions} locally within a state, avoiding the need
for a globally consistent function \citep{gabar}. In the cross-domain
setting this choice becomes load-bearing rather than convenient: a value
function shared across domains presupposes a common scale of goal distances
that does not exist, whereas local action rankings are scale-free. Our
value-objective ablation examines this directly.

\textbf{Large language models and planning.} LLM-based planners have been
evaluated extensively; systematic studies find that performance of large
reasoning models degrades sharply when symbols are renamed
\citep{llm_baseline}, indicating reliance on symbol identity. \gadar{} is
invariant to symbol renaming by construction. LLM code-synthesis approaches
that write per-domain programs require solved instances and validator loops
at test time, addressing a different question from zero-shot execution.
